**Summary:** In a non-binding opinion on 3 November 2026, Advocate General Maciej Szpunar told the Court of Justice of the EU that using copyrighted works to train AI is as a rule allowed under the text-and-data-mining exception, but that chatbots can infringe the press publisher's right when they access articles unlawfully or reuse their form of expression recognisably. The underlying case is a Hungarian press publisher's claim against Google's Gemini.

**Claims**
- `c1` On 2026-11-03 Advocate General Maciej Szpunar issued a non-binding opinion to the Court of Justice of the EU saying that using copyrighted works to train AI "is, as a rule, freely permitted" under the text-and-data-mining exception in EU law.
- `c2` Rapporteur says this is the first time a dispute about how far AI developers may go in their data use has reached the Court of Justice. The court is not bound by the opinion but usually follows the advocate general.
- `c3` The underlying case was brought by a Hungarian press publisher that claimed Google's Gemini chatbot could reproduce one of its articles, in breach of its copyright. Szpunar advised the court to declare the questions about that case inadmissible and said there was no evidence Google had used the publisher's articles to train its AI.
- `c4` Szpunar said chatbots can still infringe the press publisher's right when they access press articles online to generate answers, depending on whether the access was unlawful, for example by circumventing paywalls or anti-scraping measures.
- `c5` Per the opinion, merely reporting facts from an article is not an infringement, but answers that "systematically" show the original article's style of expression in a recognisable way would infringe the press publisher's right.
- `c6` Szpunar argued that the company running a chatbot is liable for such infringements if they are inherent to its business model or if it failed to take effective measures to prevent them, such as unlawful web scraping.
