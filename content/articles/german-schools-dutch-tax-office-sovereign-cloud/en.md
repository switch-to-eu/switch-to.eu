These are decisions and plans by public bodies, so your own apps stay as they are. What you can take from them is one question for your own files: who stays in control? If your children go to school in Berlin, Bremen, Hamburg, Hesse, North Rhine-Westphalia or Rhineland-Palatinate, you also have a question for the school.

The three cases give three different answers. Those six German states have agreed a framework contract for a school platform without software from Microsoft or Google, [heise reported on 3 November](https://www.heise.de/news/Nextcloud-und-Ionos-Open-Source-Bildungsplattform-fuer-sechs-Bundeslaender-11510231.html). The Dutch tax office drops Microsoft 365's cloud for servers of its own, a [letter to parliament said on 5 October](https://www.tweedekamer.nl/kamerstukken/brieven_regering/detail?id=2026Z21114&did=2026D48732). And on 2 November, at Germany's Digital Summit in Berlin, Chancellor Friedrich Merz [was asked about a reported plan](https://www.heise.de/news/Merz-Google-Cloud-ausreichend-souveraen-fuer-Nachrichtendienste-11508517.html) for the German intelligence services to build their new offline cloud with Google, and he defended it. The school deal became known on the sidelines of the same summit, and both German sides use the word sovereignty.

## If your children go to school in one of the six states

Ask the school whether it plans to use the new platform. Schools and school authorities [aren't obliged to use it](https://nextcloud.com/blog/six-german-states-gain-access-to-sovereign-collaboration-for-schools/), so the choice sits with them. Neither Nextcloud nor heise gives a number of schools signed up. What exists today is a contract and an option.

Schools that take it get IONOS Nextcloud Workspace, which covers file sharing, chat, video calls, email, calendar and office apps. [heise says](https://www.heise.de/news/Nextcloud-und-Ionos-Open-Source-Bildungsplattform-fuer-sechs-Bundeslaender-11510231.html) the platform is meant to meet GDPR requirements and protect against access by US authorities, for example under the US CLOUD Act.

The agreement is [called KoDaS](https://nextcloud.com/blog/six-german-states-gain-access-to-sovereign-collaboration-for-schools/), and other states are free to join. govdigital, a cooperative of German public IT providers, ran the procurement, so the states [don't need a new tender](https://www.heise.de/news/Nextcloud-und-Ionos-Open-Source-Bildungsplattform-fuer-sechs-Bundeslaender-11510231.html). IONOS won the four-year contract and runs the service in German data centres. Nextcloud, based in Stuttgart, supplies the open-source software.

Nextcloud has a commercial interest here. Founder Frank Karlitschek [told dpa](https://www.heise.de/news/Nextcloud-und-Ionos-Open-Source-Bildungsplattform-fuer-sechs-Bundeslaender-11510231.html) his company needs no subsidies, only public-sector customers who set an example.

## If you want more control over your own files

The school deal is for schools and school authorities, but the software is open to anyone. [Nextcloud](/services/eu/nextcloud) doesn't sell hosting itself, so you sign up at one of its partner hosts.

Partner hosts give you a free account with 2 to 5 GB. For more space you pay the host, and each host sets its own price. We'd start on a free account and compare prices before moving much.

The host you pick decides where your data is stored, not Nextcloud, so check that first. Encryption on your own device is optional and set per folder, so anything outside the folders you pick isn't covered.

Some users report a sluggish Android app, large uploads that need a retry, and sync conflicts they sort out by hand. Running Nextcloud on your own server is free, but you'll need to be comfortable with Linux.

If you're on [Google Drive](/services/non-eu/google-drive) and prefer a ready-made service, the [Google Drive to pCloud guide](/guides/storage/google-drive-to-pcloud) takes about an hour. [pCloud](/services/eu/pcloud) is based in Switzerland, keeps your files in Luxembourg if you choose the EU region at sign-up, and isn't open source. Our [storage comparison](/services/storage) sets Nextcloud next to the other European options.

## The Dutch tax office: own servers, existing licences

The Dutch Tax Administration, Customs and the Benefits Agency move to software on servers the tax office owns or controls, says the [letter from State Secretary Eelco Eerenberg](https://www.tweedekamer.nl/kamerstukken/brieven_regering/detail?id=2026Z21114&did=2026D48732). Mail and calendar move first, in 2027. European open-source tools for personal storage and collaboration follow later in 2027 and in 2028.

That's not the same as leaving Microsoft. The plan is to reuse existing licences as much as possible, to keep extra spending low. The letter doesn't say whose licences those are, or which mail software will run on the new servers.

There's a cost too. Some planned records-management features came with the Microsoft 365 package and aren't available on the office's own servers. The state secretary promises an update by the next progress letter at the latest.

Nowhere does the letter call any of this sovereign. Its word is autonomy.

The three agencies chose Microsoft 365 in 2025, and the rollout had reached [about 5,000 of 47,500 employees](https://www.theregister.com/a/5301603). In June 2026 the government's ICT review board recommended rolling back. According to The Register, the board objected to confidential documents travelling via American servers and to standard Microsoft encryption rather than a version giving more control. Lock-in, reduced sovereignty and the lack of an exit plan were also on its list.

## The German intelligence services: a US partner, offline

[heise writes](https://www.heise.de/news/Merz-Google-Cloud-ausreichend-souveraen-fuer-Nachrichtendienste-11508517.html) that the services apparently want Google as project partner, and its report names no contract. No signed contract is confirmed, and the terms aren't known.

Digital sovereignty, [Merz said in his speech](https://www.heise.de/news/Merz-Google-Cloud-ausreichend-souveraen-fuer-Nachrichtendienste-11508517.html), means Germany making and enforcing its own decisions in line with its values and security interests. He ruled out autarky, a country doing everything by itself, as the goal.

Asked about the intelligence services, he said Google invests in Germany and Europe, and that European sovereignty is possible with an American provider. He called the setup "a European infrastructure" and said he has high trust that the data will be safe. The Chancellery he heads also coordinates those services.

heise also raises the main objection: no cloud from a big US provider runs for long without updates from the vendor, and that includes the offline, air-gapped kind the German services want. If updates stopped, heise writes, a replacement would have to be found within months.

## Who holds what

Our reading of the three, side by side:

- **Schools:** IONOS holds the machines under a public contract. The code is open, and other hosts run Nextcloud too. The open question is take-up.
- **The Dutch tax office:** it holds or controls the machines itself. Still open: the records-management features and whose licences it reuses.
- **The intelligence services:** the cloud is offline and, in Merz's description, European. A US company would be the project partner, and by heise's account the system depends on its updates.

By Merz's own definition, the test for the Google plan is whether Germany still enforces its decisions if those updates stop. heise's report records no answer to that.

So far, the people saying "sovereign" are mostly those buying or selling. heise reports that Germany's cyber security agency BSI and the European Commission are working on detailed criteria for when a cloud provider meets which security level for which use. How they'll treat offline clouds from US providers is still open.
